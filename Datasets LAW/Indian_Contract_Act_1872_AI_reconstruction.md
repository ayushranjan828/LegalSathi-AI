## PRELIMINARY

### Section 1. Short title, extent, commencement

- The Act is to be cited as the Indian Contract Act, 1872.
- It applies to the whole of India. (The earlier exclusion of Jammu and Kashmir was removed in 2019.) [verify]
- It took effect on 1 September 1872.
- Saving clause: the Act does not disturb (a) any statute, Act or regulation that it does not expressly repeal, (b) any usage or custom of trade, or (c) any incident of a contract, so long as these are not inconsistent with the Act.

### Section 2. Interpretation-clause

Unless the context requires otherwise, the following words carry these meanings in the Act:

- (a) A **"proposal"** is made when one person signals to another that he is willing to do, or to refrain from doing, something, with the aim of getting that other person's assent.
- (b) When the person to whom the proposal is made signals his assent, the proposal is **"accepted"**. A proposal once accepted becomes a **"promise"**.
- (c) The person making the proposal is the **"promisor"**; the person accepting it is the **"promisee"**.
- (d) **"Consideration"**: when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or refrained from doing something, or does or refrains from doing it, or promises to do or refrain from doing it, that act, abstinence or promise is the consideration for the promise.
- (e) Every promise, and every set of promises that are consideration for each other, is an **"agreement"**.
- (f) Promises that form the consideration (or part of it) for each other are **"reciprocal promises"**.
- (g) An agreement that the law will not enforce is **"void"**.
- (h) An agreement the law will enforce is a **"contract"**.
- (i) An agreement that is enforceable at the option of one or more parties but not at the option of the other or others is a **"voidable contract"**.
- (j) A contract that stops being legally enforceable becomes **"void"** at the moment it stops being enforceable.

## CHAPTER I — OF THE COMMUNICATION, ACCEPTANCE AND REVOCATION OF PROPOSALS

### Section 3. Communication, acceptance and revocation of proposals

A proposal, its acceptance and the revocation of either are treated as communicated by any act or omission of the party concerned by which he intends to communicate it, or which in fact has the effect of communicating it.

### Section 4. Communication when complete

- **Proposal:** communication is complete when it reaches the knowledge of the person to whom it is made.
- **Acceptance:**
  - as against the proposer — complete once it is sent on its way to him so that it is beyond the acceptor's power to recall;
  - as against the acceptor — complete when it reaches the proposer's knowledge.
- **Revocation:**
  - as against the person revoking — complete once it is sent so that it is beyond his power to recall;
  - as against the person to whom it is made — complete when it reaches his knowledge.

*Example:* A posts a letter offering to sell his house to B. The offer is communicated when B receives the letter. If B posts an acceptance, A is bound once B's letter is posted; B is bound only when A receives it.

### Section 5. Revocation of proposals and acceptances

- A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not later.
- An acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not later.

*Example:* In the posted-letter scenario, A may withdraw his offer any time before B posts his acceptance; B may withdraw his acceptance any time before his acceptance letter reaches A.

### Section 6. Revocation how made

A proposal is revoked:

- (1) when the proposer communicates notice of revocation to the other party;
- (2) when the time fixed in the proposal for acceptance runs out, or, if no time is fixed, when a reasonable time passes, without acceptance being communicated;
- (3) when the acceptor fails to satisfy a condition that had to be met before acceptance; or
- (4) on the death or insanity of the proposer, if the acceptor learns of it before accepting.

### Section 7. Acceptance must be absolute

To turn a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must:

- (1) be absolute and unqualified; and
- (2) be expressed in some usual and reasonable manner, unless the proposal specifies how it is to be accepted.

If the proposal prescribes a manner of acceptance and the acceptance does not follow it, the proposer may, within a reasonable time after the acceptance reaches him, insist that it be made in the prescribed manner. If he does not do so, he is treated as having accepted the acceptance.

### Section 8. Acceptance by performing conditions, or receiving consideration

Performing the conditions of a proposal, or accepting any consideration offered with a reciprocal promise, amounts to acceptance of the proposal.

### Section 9. Promises, express and implied

A promise is **express** to the extent that the proposal or acceptance is made in words. It is **implied** to the extent that the proposal or acceptance is made otherwise than in words.

## CHAPTER II — OF CONTRACTS, VOIDABLE CONTRACTS AND VOID AGREEMENTS

### Section 10. What agreements are contracts

An agreement is a contract if it is made by the **free consent** of parties **competent to contract**, for a **lawful consideration** and with a **lawful object**, and is not expressly declared void by this Act.

This does not override any law in force in India (and not expressly repealed) that requires a particular contract to be in writing, to be made before witnesses, or to be registered.

### Section 11. Who are competent to contract

A person is competent to contract if he:

- has reached the age of majority under the law that applies to him;
- is of sound mind; and
- is not disqualified from contracting by any law to which he is subject.

### Section 12. What is a sound mind for the purposes of contracting

- A person is of sound mind for contracting if, when he makes the contract, he can understand it and form a rational judgment about how it affects his interests.
- A person who is usually of unsound mind but occasionally sound may contract while he is of sound mind.
- A person who is usually of sound mind but occasionally unsound may not contract while he is of unsound mind.

*Example:* A patient in a mental hospital who has lucid intervals may validly contract during such an interval; a normally sane person who is delirious with fever, or so drunk that he cannot understand the terms, cannot contract while in that state.

### Section 13. "Consent" defined

Two or more persons consent when they agree on the same thing in the same sense.

### Section 14. "Free consent" defined

Consent is free when it is not caused by:

- (1) coercion (s. 15);
- (2) undue influence (s. 16);
- (3) fraud (s. 17);
- (4) misrepresentation (s. 18); or
- (5) mistake, subject to ss. 20, 21 and 22.

Consent is "caused" by any of these when it would not have been given without it.

### Section 15. "Coercion" defined

**Coercion** means committing, or threatening to commit, any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or unlawfully detaining, or threatening to detain, any property, to the prejudice of any person, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.

*Explanation:* It does not matter whether the Indian Penal Code is in force at the place where the coercion is used.

*Example:* On a ship on the high seas, A makes B sign an agreement by threatening conduct that would be criminal intimidation under the Penal Code. Later sued in India, A cannot escape the charge of coercion merely because the act was not committed where the Code applies.

### Section 16. "Undue influence" defined

- (1) A contract is induced by **undue influence** where the relations between the parties are such that one is in a position to dominate the will of the other, and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over him.
- (2) In particular, and without limiting (1), a person is considered to be in a position to dominate another's will where he:
  - (a) holds real or apparent authority over the other, or stands in a fiduciary relation to him; or
  - (b) contracts with a person whose mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected by age, illness, or mental or bodily distress.
- (3) Where a person in a position to dominate another's will enters into a contract with him, and the transaction looks unconscionable on its face or on the evidence, the burden of proving that it was not induced by undue influence lies on the dominant person. This does not affect s. 111 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.

*Example:* A spiritual adviser persuades his devotee to transfer property to him in return for promised spiritual benefits; the relation of dominance and the unfair bargain suggest undue influence, and the adviser must prove otherwise.

### Section 17. "Fraud" defined

**Fraud** covers any of the following acts done by a party to a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive the other party or his agent, or to induce him to enter into the contract:

- (1) asserting as a fact something untrue by one who does not believe it to be true;
- (2) actively concealing a fact by one who knows or believes it;
- (3) making a promise with no intention of keeping it;
- (4) any other act designed to deceive;
- (5) any act or omission that the law specially declares fraudulent.

*Explanation:* Mere silence about facts likely to affect a person's willingness to contract is not fraud, unless the circumstances impose a duty on the silent person to speak, or the silence is itself equivalent to speech.

*Example:* A sells a horse at auction to B, knowing it is unsound, and says nothing — not fraud. But if B is A's daughter who has just come of age and relies on him, A's silence may be fraud. If B asks A whether the horse is sound and A says nothing, his silence amounts to speech.

### Section 18. "Misrepresentation" defined

**Misrepresentation** means and includes:

- (1) a positive statement, made in a way not justified by the information of the person making it, of something untrue, even though he believes it to be true;
- (2) any breach of duty, without intent to deceive, that gives an advantage to the person committing it (or anyone claiming under him) by misleading another to his prejudice or to the prejudice of anyone claiming under him;
- (3) causing a party to an agreement, however innocently, to make a mistake about the substance of the thing that is the subject of the agreement.

### Section 19. Voidability of agreements without free consent

- When consent to an agreement is caused by **coercion, fraud or misrepresentation**, the agreement is a contract **voidable** at the option of the party whose consent was so caused.
- A party whose consent was caused by fraud or misrepresentation may, if he prefers, insist that the contract be performed and that he be placed in the position he would have been in had the representations been true.

*Exception:* If consent was caused by misrepresentation, or by silence that is fraudulent within s. 17, the contract is not voidable if the party could have discovered the truth with ordinary diligence.

*Explanation:* Fraud or misrepresentation that did not cause the consent does not make the contract voidable.

*Example:* A falsely tells B that his land yields a certain quantity of produce, intending to deceive. B buys relying on this. B may avoid the contract, or may insist on it being performed and the representation being made good.

### Section 19A. Power to set aside contract induced by undue influence

A contract whose consent was caused by **undue influence** is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. The Court may set it aside absolutely, or, where that party has received some benefit, on such terms and conditions as the Court considers just.

*Example:* A moneylender, taking advantage of B's financial distress, lends Rs. 100 on a bond for Rs. 200 at an excessive rate. The Court may set the bond aside on terms that B repays the money actually received with reasonable interest.

### Section 20. Agreement void where both parties are under mistake as to matter of fact

Where both parties are mistaken about a matter of fact that is essential to the agreement, the agreement is void.

*Explanation:* A mistaken opinion about the value of the thing that is the subject of the agreement is not a mistake of fact.

*Example:* A agrees to sell B a cargo believed to be on its way from England; unknown to both, the ship had already been lost and the cargo destroyed. The agreement is void.

### Section 21. Effect of mistakes as to law

A contract is not voidable because it was caused by a mistake about any law in force in India. A mistake about a law not in force in India has the same effect as a mistake of fact.

### Section 22. Contract caused by mistake of one party as to matter of fact

A contract is not voidable merely because one of the parties was mistaken about a matter of fact.

### Section 23. What considerations and objects are lawful, and what not

The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful unless:

- it is forbidden by law; or
- it is of such a nature that, if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law; or
- it is fraudulent; or
- it involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or
- the Court regards it as immoral or opposed to public policy.

In each of these cases the consideration or object is unlawful, and every agreement with an unlawful object or consideration is **void**.

*Example:* A promises to pay B to drop a prosecution that B has started against A for robbery. The agreement is void because its object is to stifle a criminal prosecution.

### Section 24. Agreements void, if considerations and objects unlawful in part

If any part of a single consideration for one or more objects, or any one or part of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the whole agreement is void.

*Example:* A promises to supervise B's lawful indigo business and also an unlawful trade, for a single annual salary. The agreement is void, since the salary is consideration for both.

### Section 25. Agreement without consideration, void, unless it is in writing and registered, or is a promise to compensate for something done, or is a promise to pay a debt barred by limitation law

An agreement made without consideration is void, unless:

- (1) it is in writing and registered under the law for the time being in force for registering documents, and is made on account of natural love and affection between parties standing in a near relation to each other; or
- (2) it is a promise to compensate, wholly or partly, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or done something the promisor was legally compellable to do; or
- (3) it is a promise, in writing and signed by the person to be charged or his authorised agent (general or special), to pay wholly or partly a debt that the creditor could have enforced but for the law of limitation.

In any of these cases the agreement is a contract.

- *Explanation 1:* Nothing here affects the validity, as between donor and donee, of a gift actually made.
- *Explanation 2:* An agreement to which consent is freely given is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate; but inadequacy may be considered in deciding whether consent was freely given.

*Example:* A finds B's purse and returns it; B later promises to pay A Rs. 50. This is a contract under (2). A written, signed promise by B to pay a time-barred debt of Rs. 1,000 is a contract under (3).

### Section 26. Agreement in restraint of marriage, void

Every agreement that restrains the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void.

### Section 27. Agreement in restraint of trade, void

Every agreement that restrains anyone from carrying on a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind is void to that extent.

*Exception 1 — Saving of agreement not to carry on business of which goodwill is sold:* A person who sells the goodwill of a business may agree with the buyer not to carry on a similar business within specified local limits, so long as the buyer (or anyone deriving title to the goodwill from him) carries on a like business there, provided the Court considers the limits reasonable having regard to the nature of the business.

(Exceptions 2 and 3, relating to partners, were repealed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.)

### Section 28. Agreements in restraint of legal proceedings, void

Every agreement is void to the extent that it:

- (a) absolutely restricts a party from enforcing his rights under or in respect of a contract by the usual legal proceedings in the ordinary tribunals, or limits the time within which he may so enforce them; or
- (b) extinguishes a party's rights, or discharges a party from liability, under or in respect of a contract on the expiry of a specified period, so as to restrict any party from enforcing his rights.

*Exception 1 — Saving of contract to refer to arbitration disputes that may arise:* An agreement by two or more persons that any future dispute between them on any subject will be referred to arbitration, and that only the amount awarded will be recoverable, is not illegal.

*Exception 2 — Saving of contract to refer questions that have already arisen:* A written contract to refer to arbitration a question that has already arisen between the parties is not illegal, and this section does not affect any law in force relating to references to arbitration.

*Exception 3 — Saving of a guarantee agreement of a bank or a financial institution:* A written contract by which a bank or financial institution includes in a guarantee a term extinguishing rights or discharging liability on the expiry of a specified period of not less than one year from the date on which a specified event occurs or does not occur, is not illegal. [verify]

*Explanation:* "Bank" and "financial institution" here refer to the institutions identified in the section by reference to the banking and financial-institution statutes (e.g. banking companies, corresponding new banks, State Bank of India and its subsidiaries, regional rural banks, co-operative banks, multi-State co-operative banks, and public financial institutions under the Companies Act). [verify]

### Section 29. Agreements void for uncertainty

Agreements whose meaning is not certain, or cannot be made certain, are void.

*Example:* A agrees to sell B "a hundred tons of oil", without anything showing which oil is meant. The agreement is void for uncertainty. If A is known to deal only in coconut oil, the meaning can be made certain and the agreement is valid.

### Section 30. Agreements by way of wager, void

Agreements by way of wager are void. No suit may be brought to recover anything allegedly won on a wager, or entrusted to any person to abide the result of any game or other uncertain event on which a wager is made.

*Exception in favour of certain prizes for horse-racing:* This section does not make unlawful a subscription or contribution, or an agreement to subscribe or contribute, made towards any plate, prize or sum of money of the value of five hundred rupees or more, to be awarded to the winner or winners of any horse-race.

*Section 294A of the Indian Penal Code not affected:* Nothing in this section legalises any transaction connected with horse-racing to which s. 294A of the Indian Penal Code applies.

## CHAPTER III — OF CONTINGENT CONTRACTS

### Section 31. "Contingent contract" defined

A **contingent contract** is a contract to do or not to do something if some event, collateral to the contract, does or does not happen.

*Example:* A contracts to pay B Rs. 10,000 if B's house is burnt. This is a contingent contract.

### Section 32. Enforcement of contracts contingent on an event happening

A contract contingent on a future uncertain event happening cannot be enforced unless and until that event has happened. If the event becomes impossible, the contract becomes void.

*Example:* A agrees to buy B's horse if A survives C. This cannot be enforced unless C dies in A's lifetime.

### Section 33. Enforcement of contracts contingent on an event not happening

A contract contingent on a future uncertain event not happening can be enforced once that event becomes impossible, and not before.

*Example:* A agrees to pay B if a certain ship does not return. If the ship sinks, the contract becomes enforceable.

### Section 34. When event on which contract is contingent to be deemed impossible, if it is the future conduct of a living person

If the future event is the way a person will act at an unspecified time, the event is treated as impossible when that person does anything that makes it impossible for him to act that way within any definite time, or otherwise than under further contingencies.

*Example:* A agrees to pay B if B marries C. C marries D. B's marriage to C is now treated as impossible, although D might die and C might later marry B.

### Section 35. When contracts become void which are contingent on happening of specified event within fixed time

- A contract contingent on a specified uncertain event happening within a fixed time becomes void if, when the time expires, the event has not happened, or if before then the event becomes impossible.
- **When contracts may be enforced which are contingent on specified event not happening within fixed time:** A contract contingent on a specified uncertain event not happening within a fixed time may be enforced once the time has expired without the event happening, or earlier if it becomes certain that the event will not happen.

*Example:* A promises to pay B if a certain ship returns within a year. The contract may be enforced if the ship returns within the year, and becomes void if the ship is burnt within the year.

### Section 36. Agreement contingent on impossible events void

A contingent agreement to do or not to do something if an impossible event happens is void, whether or not the parties knew of the impossibility when the agreement was made.

*Example:* A agrees to pay B if two straight lines enclose a space. The agreement is void.

## CHAPTER IV — OF THE PERFORMANCE OF CONTRACTS

#### Contracts which must be performed

### Section 37. Obligation of parties to contracts

The parties must either perform, or offer to perform, their respective promises, unless performance is dispensed with or excused under this Act or any other law.

Promises bind the promisors' representatives if the promisor dies before performance, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract.

*Example:* A promises to deliver goods to B on payment. A dies before the day. A's representatives must deliver the goods and B must pay them. But if A had promised to paint a picture for B, the contract is personal and ends with A's death.

### Section 38. Effect of refusal to accept offer of performance

Where a promisor has offered to perform to the promisee and the offer is not accepted, the promisor is not responsible for non-performance and does not lose his rights under the contract.

Such an offer must satisfy these conditions:

- (1) it must be unconditional;
- (2) it must be made at a proper time and place, and in circumstances that give the promisee a reasonable opportunity to see that the offeror is able and willing then and there to do all he is bound to do;
- (3) if the offer is to deliver something, the promisee must have a reasonable opportunity to see that the thing offered is the thing the promisor is bound to deliver.

An offer to one of several joint promisees has the same legal effect as an offer to all of them.

### Section 39. Effect of refusal of party to perform promise wholly

When a party has refused to perform, or has disabled himself from performing, his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract, unless he has signalled by words or conduct that he accepts its continuance.

*Example:* A, a singer, agrees to sing at B's theatre for two nights a week for two months, and B agrees to pay per night. On the sixth night A wilfully absents herself. B may end the contract; but if he lets her sing on the seventh night, he cannot later terminate on that ground, though he can claim compensation.

#### By whom contracts must be performed

### Section 40. Person by whom promise is to be performed

If the nature of the case shows that the parties intended the promise to be performed by the promisor personally, he must perform it himself. In other cases, the promisor or his representatives may employ a competent person to perform it.

### Section 41. Effect of accepting performance from third person

When a promisee accepts performance of the promise from a third person, he cannot afterwards enforce it against the promisor.

### Section 42. Devolution of joint liabilities

When two or more persons have made a joint promise, then, unless the contract shows a contrary intention, all of them must perform it during their joint lives; after the death of any of them, his representative together with the survivors must perform; and after the death of the last survivor, the representatives of all of them jointly must perform.

### Section 43. Any one of joint promisors may be compelled to perform

- In the absence of an express agreement to the contrary, the promisee may compel any one or more of the joint promisors to perform the whole promise.
- **Each promisor may compel contribution:** each joint promisor may require every other joint promisor to contribute equally to the performance, unless the contract shows a contrary intention.
- **Sharing of loss by default in contribution:** if any joint promisor fails to contribute, the others must bear the resulting loss in equal shares.

*Explanation:* This does not stop a surety from recovering from his principal what he has paid on the principal's behalf, nor does it allow the principal to recover anything from the surety on account of payments made by the principal.

*Example:* A, B and C jointly promise to pay D Rs. 3,000. D may compel any of them to pay the whole; the one who pays can recover equal shares from the others, and if one of them is insolvent, the remaining two bear the shortfall equally.

### Section 44. Effect of release of one joint promisor

Where two or more persons have made a joint promise, the promisee's release of one of them does not discharge the others, nor does it free the released promisor from his liability to the other joint promisors.

### Section 45. Devolution of joint rights

When a person has made a promise to two or more persons jointly, then, unless the contract shows a contrary intention, the right to claim performance belongs (as between him and them) to them jointly during their joint lives; after the death of any of them, to his representative jointly with the survivors; and after the death of the last survivor, to the representatives of all jointly.

#### Time and place for performance

### Section 46. Time for performance of promise, where no application is to be made and no time is specified

Where the promisor is to perform without being asked by the promisee and no time is fixed, the promise must be performed within a reasonable time.

*Explanation:* What is a reasonable time is a question of fact in each case.

### Section 47. Time and place for performance of promise, where time is specified and no application to be made

Where a promise is to be performed on a certain day and the promisor has undertaken to perform without application by the promisee, he may perform at any time during the usual hours of business on that day, at the place where the promise ought to be performed.

*Example:* A promises to deliver goods at B's warehouse on 1 January. He must bring them there on that day during usual business hours.

### Section 48. Application for performance on certain day to be at proper time and place

Where a promise is to be performed on a certain day and the promisor has not undertaken to perform without application, it is the promisee's duty to apply for performance at a proper place and within the usual hours of business.

*Explanation:* What is the proper time and place is a question of fact in each case.

### Section 49. Place for the performance of promise, where no application to be made and no place fixed for performance

Where the promisor is to perform without application and no place is fixed, the promisor must ask the promisee to appoint a reasonable place for performance, and perform there.

*Example:* A undertakes to deliver goods to B on a fixed day. A must ask B to appoint a reasonable place and deliver there.

### Section 50. Performance in manner or at time prescribed or sanctioned by promisee

A promise may be performed in any manner, or at any time, that the promisee prescribes or approves.

*Example:* B owes A money. A asks B to pay it into A's account with a banker C. B, who also banks with C, instructs C to transfer the sum, and C does so. This is good payment, even if C later fails before A draws the money.

#### Performance of reciprocal promises

### Section 51. Promisor not bound to perform, unless reciprocal promisee ready and willing to perform

Where a contract consists of reciprocal promises to be performed simultaneously, a promisor need not perform unless the promisee is ready and willing to perform his reciprocal promise.

*Example:* A agrees to sell goods to B for cash on delivery. A need not deliver unless B is ready and willing to pay; B need not pay unless A is ready and willing to deliver.

### Section 52. Order of performance of reciprocal promises

Where the contract expressly fixes the order in which reciprocal promises are to be performed, they must be performed in that order. Where it does not, they must be performed in the order the nature of the transaction requires.

*Example:* A and B agree that A will build a house for B at a fixed price. A's promise to build must be performed before B's promise to pay.

### Section 53. Liability of party preventing event on which the contract is to take effect

When a contract contains reciprocal promises and one party prevents the other from performing, the contract becomes **voidable** at the option of the party prevented, and he is entitled to compensation from the other for any loss suffered because of the non-performance.

### Section 54. Effect of default as to that promise which should be first performed, in contract consisting of reciprocal promises

Where reciprocal promises are such that one cannot be performed, or its performance cannot be claimed, until the other has been performed, and the promisor of the promise to be performed first fails to perform it, he cannot claim performance of the reciprocal promise, and must compensate the other party for any loss caused by the non-performance.

*Example:* A hires B's ship to carry cargo, agreeing to supply the cargo. A fails to supply it. A cannot claim the carriage and must compensate B for loss.

### Section 55. Effect of failure to perform at fixed time, in contract in which time is essential

- Where a party promises to do something at or before a specified time and fails to do so, the contract (or so much of it as is unperformed) becomes **voidable** at the promisee's option, if the parties intended time to be of the essence.
- **Effect of such failure when time is not essential:** if time was not intended to be of the essence, the contract does not become voidable, but the promisee is entitled to compensation for loss caused by the failure.
- **Effect of acceptance of performance at time other than that agreed upon:** if, in a contract voidable for this reason, the promisee accepts performance at a time other than the agreed one, he cannot claim compensation for loss from the delay unless he gives the promisor notice, at the time of accepting, that he intends to do so.

### Section 56. Agreement to do impossible act

- An agreement to do an act that is impossible in itself is void.
- **Contract to do act afterwards becoming impossible or unlawful:** a contract to do an act that, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or (because of an event the promisor could not prevent) unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful.
- **Compensation for loss through non-performance of act known to be impossible or unlawful:** where a person promised to do something he knew, or with reasonable diligence could have known, and the promisee did not know, to be impossible or unlawful, he must compensate the promisee for loss caused by the non-performance.

*Example:* A contract to sing at a theatre becomes void if the singer later falls so ill that she cannot perform. A man who promises to marry B while already married, and forbidden by his personal law to take a second wife, must compensate B for the loss caused by non-performance.

### Section 57. Reciprocal promise to do things legal, and also other things illegal

Where persons reciprocally promise, first, to do certain lawful things and, second, under specified circumstances, to do certain other unlawful things, the first set of promises is a contract and the second is a void agreement.

*Example:* A agrees to sell B a house for Rs. 10,000, but if B uses it as a gambling house, B is to pay Rs. 50,000. The first part is a contract; the second is void.

### Section 58. Alternative promise, one branch being illegal

In an alternative promise where one branch is lawful and the other unlawful, only the lawful branch can be enforced.

*Example:* A and B agree that A will pay B Rs. 1,000, for which B will afterwards deliver either rice or smuggled opium. This is a valid contract to deliver rice and a void agreement as to the opium.

#### Appropriation of payments

### Section 59. Application of payment where debt to be discharged is indicated

Where a debtor owing several distinct debts to one person pays him money with an express statement, or in circumstances implying, that the payment is for a particular debt, the payment, if accepted, must be applied accordingly.

### Section 60. Application of payment where debt to be discharged is not indicated

Where the debtor does not indicate, and there are no other circumstances indicating, which debt the payment is for, the creditor may apply it at his discretion to any lawful debt actually due and payable to him by the debtor, whether or not its recovery is time-barred.

### Section 61. Application of payment where neither party appropriates

Where neither party makes an appropriation, the payment is applied to the debts in order of time, whether or not they are time-barred. If the debts are of equal standing, the payment is applied to each proportionately.

#### Contracts which need not be performed

### Section 62. Effect of novation, rescission, and alteration of contract

If the parties agree to substitute a new contract for it, or to rescind or alter it, the original contract need not be performed.

*Example:* A owes B money under a contract. A, B and C agree that B will accept C as debtor instead of A. A's old debt is discharged and a new debt from C to B is created.

### Section 63. Promisee may dispense with or remit performance of promise

Every promisee may dispense with or remit, wholly or partly, the performance of the promise made to him, may extend the time for performance, or may accept any satisfaction he thinks fit in place of it.

*Example:* A owes B Rs. 5,000. A pays and B accepts Rs. 2,000 in satisfaction of the whole debt at the time and place where the debt was payable. The whole debt is discharged.

### Section 64. Consequences of rescission of voidable contract

When a person at whose option a contract is voidable rescinds it, the other party need not perform any promise in it of which he is the promisor. The party rescinding must, so far as possible, restore any benefit he has received under the contract to the person from whom he received it.

### Section 65. Obligation of person who has received advantage under void agreement, or contract that becomes void

When an agreement is discovered to be void, or a contract becomes void, any person who has received an advantage under it must restore it, or compensate for it, to the person from whom he received it.

*Example:* A pays B Rs. 1,000 in return for B's promise to marry A's daughter, C, who was dead at the time of the promise. The agreement is void, and B must repay the Rs. 1,000.

### Section 66. Mode of communicating or revoking rescission of voidable contract

Rescission of a voidable contract may be communicated or revoked in the same way, and subject to the same rules, as apply to communicating or revoking a proposal.

### Section 67. Effect of neglect of promisee to afford promisor reasonable facilities for performance

If a promisee neglects or refuses to give the promisor reasonable facilities for performing his promise, the promisor is excused for any non-performance caused by that neglect or refusal.

*Example:* A contracts to repair B's house. B neglects to point out the places needing repair. A is excused for failing to repair them.

## CHAPTER V — OF CERTAIN RELATIONS RESEMBLING THOSE CREATED BY CONTRACT

### Section 68. Claim for necessaries supplied to person incapable of contracting, or on his account

If a person who is incapable of contracting, or anyone he is legally bound to support, is supplied by another with necessaries suited to his condition in life, the supplier is entitled to be reimbursed out of the property of the incapable person.

*Example:* A supplies B, a lunatic, with necessaries suited to his station. A may be reimbursed from B's property.

### Section 69. Reimbursement of person paying money due by another, in payment of which he is interested

A person who is interested in the payment of money that another is legally bound to pay, and who pays it for that reason, is entitled to be reimbursed by the other.

*Example:* B holds land on lease from a zamindar; the revenue due by the zamindar is in arrears and the land is advertised for sale. B pays the arrears to prevent the sale. The zamindar must reimburse B.

### Section 70. Obligation of person enjoying benefit of non-gratuitous act

Where a person lawfully does something for another, or delivers something to him, not intending to do so gratuitously, and the other enjoys the benefit of it, the other must compensate him for it, or restore the thing done or delivered.

*Example:* A, a tradesman, leaves goods at B's house by mistake. B treats them as his own. B must pay A for them.

### Section 71. Responsibility of finder of goods

A person who finds goods belonging to another and takes them into his custody has the same responsibility as a **bailee**.

### Section 72. Liability of person to whom money is paid, or thing delivered, by mistake or under coercion

A person to whom money has been paid, or anything delivered, by mistake or under coercion, must repay or return it.

*Example:* A railway company refuses to deliver goods to the consignee except on payment of an illegal charge. The consignee pays to get the goods. He may recover the excess charge.

## CHAPTER VI — OF THE CONSEQUENCES OF BREACH OF CONTRACT

### Section 73. Compensation for loss or damage caused by breach of contract

- When a contract is broken, the party who suffers is entitled to receive from the party in breach compensation for any loss or damage caused to him that either arose naturally in the usual course of things from the breach, or that the parties knew, when they made the contract, would be likely to result from the breach.
- No compensation is given for any **remote and indirect** loss or damage caused by the breach.
- **Compensation for failure to discharge obligation resembling those created by contract:** when an obligation resembling one created by contract has been incurred and is not discharged, the injured person is entitled to the same compensation as if the defaulting person had contracted to discharge it and broken that contract.

*Explanation:* In estimating the loss or damage, account must be taken of the means that existed of remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance.

*Example:* A contracts to sell and deliver saltpetre to B at a set price on a future day. A breaks the contract. B is entitled to the difference between the contract price and the market price on the day of delivery.

### Section 74. Compensation for breach of contract where penalty stipulated for

When a contract has been broken, if the contract names a sum to be paid in case of breach, or contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the complaining party is entitled — whether or not actual damage or loss is proved — to receive from the party in breach **reasonable compensation** not exceeding the sum named or the penalty stipulated.

*Explanation:* A stipulation for increased interest from the date of default may be a stipulation by way of penalty.

*Exception:* A person who enters into a bail-bond, recognisance or similar instrument, or who, under any law or under orders of the Central or a State Government, gives a bond for the performance of a public duty or an act in which the public is interested, is liable on breach of its condition to pay the whole sum mentioned in it.

*Explanation:* A person who contracts with the Government does not, merely by doing so, undertake a public duty or promise to do an act in which the public is interested.

*Example:* A contracts with B to pay Rs. 1,000 if he fails to pay B Rs. 500 on a given day. A fails. B can recover from A only such compensation, not exceeding Rs. 1,000, as the Court considers reasonable.

### Section 75. Party rightfully rescinding contract entitled to compensation

A person who rightfully rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation for any damage he has suffered through the non-fulfilment of the contract.

*Example:* A, a singer, contracts with B to sing at his theatre for two months, and B agrees to pay monthly. A then refuses to perform. B may rescind the contract and claim compensation for the damage caused.

## CHAPTER VIII — OF INDEMNITY AND GUARANTEE

### Section 124. "Contract of indemnity" defined

A **contract of indemnity** is a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself or of any other person.

*Example:* A agrees to indemnify B against the consequences of any proceedings C may take against B over a certain sum of money. This is a contract of indemnity.

### Section 125. Rights of indemnity-holder when sued

The promisee in a contract of indemnity, acting within the scope of his authority, is entitled to recover from the promisor:

- (1) all damages he may be compelled to pay in any suit about any matter covered by the promise to indemnify;
- (2) all costs he may be compelled to pay in any such suit, if in bringing or defending it he did not disobey the promisor's orders and acted as it would have been prudent to act without the indemnity, or if the promisor authorised him to bring or defend the suit;
- (3) all sums he may have paid under the terms of a compromise of any such suit, if the compromise was not contrary to the promisor's orders and was one a prudent person would have made without the indemnity, or if the promisor authorised him to compromise.

### Section 126. "Contract of guarantee", "surety", "principal debtor" and "creditor"

- A **contract of guarantee** is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person if he defaults.
- The person giving the guarantee is the **surety**; the person for whose default it is given is the **principal debtor**; the person to whom it is given is the **creditor**.
- A guarantee may be oral or written.

### Section 127. Consideration for guarantee

Anything done, or any promise made, for the benefit of the principal debtor may be sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee.

*Example:* B asks A to sell and deliver goods on credit to C and promises to pay if C does not. A delivers on that promise. This is sufficient consideration for B's guarantee. But if A has already sold and delivered goods to C, and B later, without any consideration, promises to pay if C does not, B's promise is void.

### Section 128. Surety's liability

Unless the contract provides otherwise, the surety's liability is **co-extensive** with that of the principal debtor.

*Example:* A guarantees payment of a bill of exchange by B, the acceptor. The bill is dishonoured. A is liable not only for the amount of the bill but also for any interest and charges due on it.

### Section 129. "Continuing guarantee"

A guarantee that extends to a series of transactions is a **continuing guarantee**.

*Example:* A, in return for B agreeing to employ C to collect B's rents, promises to be responsible up to Rs. 5,000 for C's due collection and payment. This is a continuing guarantee.

### Section 130. Revocation of continuing guarantee

The surety may revoke a continuing guarantee at any time, as to future transactions, by giving notice to the creditor.

*Example:* A guarantees to B up to Rs. 10,000 for goods B may supply to C. B supplies goods worth Rs. 5,000; A then revokes. A remains liable for those Rs. 5,000, but not for later supplies.

### Section 131. Revocation of continuing guarantee by surety's death

Unless the contract says otherwise, the death of the surety revokes a continuing guarantee as regards future transactions.

### Section 132. Liability of two persons, primarily liable, not affected by arrangement between them that one shall be surety on other's default

Where two persons contract with a third to undertake a liability, and also agree between themselves that one will be liable only if the other defaults (the third person not being a party to that second agreement), each remains liable to the third person under the first contract, even if the third person knew of the second agreement.

### Section 133. Discharge of surety by variance in terms of contract

Any change in the terms of the contract between the principal debtor and the creditor, made without the surety's consent, discharges the surety as to transactions after the change.

*Example:* A guarantees C's conduct as B's clerk at a fixed salary. Later, without A's knowledge or consent, B and C agree that C's pay will be partly by commission. A is not liable for C's later misconduct.

### Section 134. Discharge of surety by release or discharge of principal debtor

The surety is discharged by any contract between the creditor and the principal debtor releasing the principal debtor, or by any act or omission of the creditor whose legal consequence is the discharge of the principal debtor.

### Section 135. Discharge of surety when creditor compounds with, gives time to, or agrees not to sue, principal debtor

A contract between the creditor and the principal debtor by which the creditor makes a composition with, promises to give time to, or promises not to sue, the principal debtor discharges the surety, unless the surety consents to it.

### Section 136. Surety not discharged when agreement made with third person to give time to principal debtor

Where the creditor's contract to give time to the principal debtor is made with a third person and not with the principal debtor, the surety is not discharged.

### Section 137. Creditor's forbearance to sue does not discharge surety

The creditor's mere forbearance to sue the principal debtor, or to enforce any other remedy against him, does not discharge the surety, unless the guarantee provides otherwise.

*Example:* B owes C a debt guaranteed by A. The debt becomes payable. C does not sue B for a year. A is not discharged.

### Section 138. Release of one co-surety does not discharge others

Where there are co-sureties, the creditor's release of one of them does not discharge the others, nor does it free the released surety from his responsibility to the other sureties.

### Section 139. Discharge of surety by creditor's act or omission impairing surety's eventual remedy

If the creditor does any act inconsistent with the surety's rights, or omits to do an act his duty to the surety requires, and the surety's eventual remedy against the principal debtor is thereby impaired, the surety is discharged.

*Example:* B contracts to build a ship for C, payments to be made by instalments as the work progresses, and A guarantees B's performance. C pays the last two instalments in advance without A's knowledge. A is discharged by this prepayment.

### Section 140. Rights of surety on payment or performance

Where a guaranteed debt has become due, or the principal debtor has defaulted on a guaranteed duty, the surety, on paying or performing all that he is liable for, acquires all the rights the creditor had against the principal debtor.

### Section 141. Surety's right to benefit of creditor's securities

The surety is entitled to the benefit of every security the creditor holds against the principal debtor at the time the contract of suretyship is made, whether or not the surety knows of it. If the creditor loses, or parts with without the surety's consent, any such security, the surety is discharged to the extent of its value.

*Example:* C lends B money on A's guarantee, and also takes a mortgage of B's furniture. C cancels the mortgage. B becomes insolvent and C sues A. A is discharged to the extent of the furniture's value.

### Section 142. Guarantee obtained by misrepresentation invalid

A guarantee obtained through a misrepresentation made by the creditor, or with his knowledge and assent, about a material part of the transaction is invalid.

### Section 143. Guarantee obtained by concealment invalid

A guarantee obtained by the creditor by keeping silent about material circumstances is invalid.

*Example:* A employs B as a clerk to collect money; B fails to account for some receipts, and A asks him to furnish security. C guarantees B's accounting. A does not tell C about B's earlier misconduct. B later defaults. The guarantee is invalid.

### Section 144. Guarantee on contract that creditor shall not act on it until co-surety joins

If a person gives a guarantee on the understanding that the creditor will not act on it until another person joins as co-surety, the guarantee is not valid if that other person does not join.

### Section 145. Implied promise to indemnify surety

Every contract of guarantee contains an implied promise by the principal debtor to indemnify the surety. The surety may recover from the principal debtor whatever he has rightfully paid under the guarantee, but not sums he has paid wrongfully.

*Example:* B owes C a sum guaranteed by A. C demands payment from A, who refuses. C sues A, who defends without reasonable grounds and has to pay the debt plus costs. A can recover the debt from B, but not the costs, since the defence was unreasonable.

### Section 146. Co-sureties liable to contribute equally

Where two or more persons are co-sureties for the same debt or duty — jointly or severally, under the same or different contracts, and whether or not they know of each other — they are, unless otherwise agreed, liable as between themselves to pay equal shares of the whole debt, or of the part left unpaid by the principal debtor.

### Section 147. Liability of co-sureties bound in different sums

Co-sureties bound in different sums must pay equally so far as the limits of their respective obligations permit.

*Example:* A, B and C are sureties for D for Rs. 10,000, Rs. 20,000 and Rs. 40,000 respectively. D defaults for Rs. 30,000. Each pays Rs. 10,000. If D defaults for Rs. 40,000, A pays Rs. 10,000 and B and C pay Rs. 15,000 each.

## CHAPTER IX — OF BAILMENT

### Section 148. "Bailment", "bailor" and "bailee" defined

A **bailment** is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, on a contract that the goods will be returned, or otherwise dealt with according to the directions of the person delivering them, once the purpose is accomplished. The person delivering the goods is the **bailor**; the person receiving them is the **bailee**.

*Explanation:* If a person already in possession of another's goods contracts to hold them as bailee, he becomes the bailee and the owner the bailor, even though the goods were not originally delivered by way of bailment.

### Section 149. Delivery to bailee how made

Delivery to the bailee may be made by doing anything that puts the goods into the possession of the intended bailee or of a person authorised to hold them on his behalf.

### Section 150. Bailor's duty to disclose faults in goods bailed

- The bailor must disclose to the bailee any faults in the goods known to him that materially interfere with their use or expose the bailee to extraordinary risks; if he does not, he is responsible for damage arising directly to the bailee from those faults.
- If the goods are bailed for hire, the bailor is responsible for such damage whether or not he knew of the faults.

*Example:* A hires a carriage from B. The carriage is unsafe, though B does not know it, and A is injured. B is responsible to A for the injury.

### Section 151. Care to be taken by bailee

In every bailment, the bailee must take as much care of the goods bailed as a person of ordinary prudence would, in similar circumstances, take of his own goods of the same bulk, quality and value.

### Section 152. Bailee when not liable for loss, etc., of thing bailed

Without a special contract, the bailee is not responsible for loss, destruction or deterioration of the thing bailed if he has taken the care described in s. 151.

### Section 153. Termination of bailment by bailee's act inconsistent with conditions

A contract of bailment is **voidable** at the bailor's option if the bailee does any act with regard to the goods that is inconsistent with the conditions of the bailment.

*Example:* A lets B a horse for his own riding. B drives it in his carriage. A may terminate the bailment.

### Section 154. Liability of bailee making unauthorized use of goods bailed

If the bailee uses the goods otherwise than according to the conditions of the bailment, he must compensate the bailor for any damage arising from or during that use.

*Example:* A lends B a horse for his own riding only. B lets C, a member of his family, ride it; C rides carefully, but the horse accidentally falls and is injured. B must compensate A.

### Section 155. Effect of mixture, with bailor's consent, of his goods with bailee's

If the bailee, with the bailor's consent, mixes the bailor's goods with his own, they have interests in the mixture in proportion to their respective shares.

### Section 156. Effect of mixture, without bailor's consent, when the goods can be separated

If the bailee, without the bailor's consent, mixes the goods with his own and they can be separated or divided, ownership stays with each party, but the bailee must bear the cost of separation or division and any damage caused by the mixing.

### Section 157. Effect of mixture, without bailor's consent, when the goods cannot be separated

If the bailee, without the bailor's consent, mixes the goods with his own so that they cannot be separated, the bailor is entitled to compensation from the bailee for the loss of the goods.

### Section 158. Repayment, by bailor, of necessary expenses

Where, under the bailment, the goods are to be kept, carried or worked on by the bailee for the bailor, and the bailee is to receive no remuneration, the bailor must repay the bailee the necessary expenses he incurs for the purpose of the bailment.

### Section 159. Restoration of goods lent gratuitously

- A person who lends a thing for use without charge may require its return at any time, even if it was lent for a specified time or purpose.
- But if the borrower, relying on the loan for a specified time or purpose, has acted so that an early return would cause him loss greater than the benefit he has actually derived from the loan, the lender who demands early return must indemnify him for the amount by which the loss exceeds that benefit.

### Section 160. Return of goods bailed, on expiration of time or accomplishment of purpose

The bailee must return the goods, or deliver them as the bailor directs, without being asked, as soon as the period of the bailment has expired or its purpose has been accomplished.

### Section 161. Bailee's responsibility when goods are not duly returned

If, through the bailee's default, the goods are not returned, delivered or tendered at the proper time, he is responsible to the bailor for any loss, destruction or deterioration of the goods from that time.

### Section 162. Termination of gratuitous bailment by death

A gratuitous bailment ends on the death of either the bailor or the bailee.

### Section 163. Bailor entitled to increase or profit from goods bailed

Unless otherwise agreed, the bailee must deliver to the bailor, or as he directs, any increase or profit that has accrued from the goods bailed.

*Example:* A leaves a cow with B to be taken care of. The cow has a calf. B must deliver the calf as well as the cow to A.

### Section 164. Bailor's responsibility to bailee

The bailor is responsible to the bailee for any loss the bailee suffers because the bailor was not entitled to make the bailment, to receive the goods back, or to give directions about them.

### Section 165. Bailment by several joint owners

If several joint owners bail goods, the bailee may, unless otherwise agreed, return them to, or as directed by, any one joint owner without the consent of all.

### Section 166. Bailee not responsible on re-delivery to bailor without title

If the bailor had no title to the goods and the bailee, in good faith, returns them to the bailor or as he directs, the bailee is not responsible to the true owner for that delivery.

### Section 167. Right of third person claiming goods bailed

If someone other than the bailor claims the goods, he may apply to the Court to stop their delivery to the bailor and to decide the question of title.

### Section 168. Right of finder of goods; may sue for specific reward offered

- A finder of goods has no right to sue the owner for compensation for the trouble and expense he voluntarily incurred to preserve the goods and trace the owner, but he may keep the goods against the owner until he receives such compensation.
- Where the owner has offered a specific reward for the return of lost goods, the finder may sue for the reward and may keep the goods until he receives it.

### Section 169. When finder of thing commonly on sale may sell it

When a thing commonly sold is lost, and the owner cannot be found with reasonable diligence, or refuses on demand to pay the finder's lawful charges, the finder may sell it:

- (1) when the thing is in danger of perishing or of losing the greater part of its value; or
- (2) when the finder's lawful charges for the thing amount to two-thirds of its value.

### Section 170. Bailee's particular lien

Where the bailee has, in line with the purpose of the bailment, rendered a service involving labour or skill on the goods, he may, unless otherwise agreed, keep the goods until he is paid due remuneration for that service.

*Example:* A gives cloth to B, a tailor, to make into a coat. B promises to deliver the coat when finished and allow three months' credit. B is not entitled to retain the coat until paid, because credit was agreed.

### Section 171. General lien of bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys and policy-brokers

Bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of a High Court and policy-brokers may, unless otherwise agreed, keep any goods bailed to them as security for a **general balance of account**. No other persons may keep goods bailed to them as security for such a balance, unless there is an express contract to that effect.

#### Bailments of pledges

### Section 172. "Pledge", "pawnor" and "pawnee" defined

A bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise is a **pledge**. The bailor here is the **pawnor** and the bailee the **pawnee**.

### Section 173. Pawnee's right of retainer

The pawnee may keep the goods pledged not only for payment of the debt or performance of the promise, but also for the interest on the debt and all necessary expenses he has incurred in keeping possession of, or preserving, the goods.

### Section 174. Pawnee not to retain for debt or promise other than that for which goods pledged. Presumption in case of subsequent advances

Unless there is a contract to that effect, the pawnee may not keep the goods for any debt or promise other than the one for which they were pledged. But such a contract is presumed, unless something shows otherwise, as regards later advances made by the pawnee.

### Section 175. Pawnee's right as to extraordinary expenses incurred

The pawnee is entitled to recover from the pawnor any extraordinary expenses he incurs to preserve the goods pledged.

### Section 176. Pawnee's right where pawnor makes default

If the pawnor fails to pay the debt, or perform the promise, at the agreed time, the pawnee may either:

- sue the pawnor on the debt or promise and keep the goods as collateral security; or
- sell the goods, after giving the pawnor **reasonable notice** of the sale.

If the sale proceeds are less than the amount due, the pawnor remains liable for the balance. If they exceed it, the pawnee must pay the surplus to the pawnor.

### Section 177. Defaulting pawnor's right to redeem

Where a time is fixed for payment or performance and the pawnor defaults, he may still redeem the goods at any later time before they are actually sold, but must then also pay any expenses arising from his default.

### Section 178. Pledge by mercantile agent

Where a mercantile agent, with the owner's consent, is in possession of goods or documents of title to goods, a pledge made by him while acting in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent is as valid as if the owner had expressly authorised it, provided the pawnee acts in good faith and does not know, at the time of the pledge, that the pawnor lacks authority to pledge.

*Explanation:* "Mercantile agent" and "documents of title" have the same meanings as in the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.

### Section 178A. Pledge by person in possession under voidable contract

Where the pawnor obtained possession of the goods under a contract voidable under s. 19 or s. 19A, and that contract has not been rescinded at the time of the pledge, the pawnee gets a good title, provided he acts in good faith and without notice of the pawnor's defective title.

### Section 179. Pledge where pawnor has only a limited interest

Where a person pledges goods in which he has only a limited interest, the pledge is valid to the extent of that interest.

#### Suits by bailees or bailors against wrong-doers

### Section 180. Suit by bailor or bailee against wrong-doer

If a third person wrongfully deprives the bailee of the use or possession of the goods, or injures them, the bailee may use the same remedies the owner could have used had there been no bailment; and either the bailor or the bailee may sue the third person for the deprivation or injury.

### Section 181. Apportionment of relief or compensation obtained by such suits

Any relief or compensation obtained in such a suit is to be dealt with between the bailor and the bailee according to their respective interests.

## CHAPTER X — AGENCY

#### Appointment and authority of agents

### Section 182. "Agent" and "principal" defined

An **agent** is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom the act is done, or who is represented, is the **principal**.

### Section 183. Who may employ agent

Any person who has reached the age of majority under the law applicable to him, and who is of sound mind, may employ an agent.

### Section 184. Who may be an agent

As between the principal and third persons, anyone may become an agent. But a person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind cannot become an agent so as to be responsible to the principal under the provisions of this Act on that subject.

### Section 185. Consideration not necessary

No consideration is needed to create an agency.

### Section 186. Agent's authority may be expressed or implied

An agent's authority may be express or implied.

### Section 187. Definitions of express and implied authority

- Authority is **express** when it is given by spoken or written words.
- Authority is **implied** when it is to be inferred from the circumstances of the case; spoken or written words, or the ordinary course of dealing, may count as circumstances of the case.

*Example:* A owns a shop in one town and lives in another, visiting the shop occasionally. B manages the shop and regularly orders goods from C in A's name for the shop, and A pays for them out of the shop's funds. B has implied authority from A to order goods from C in A's name for the shop.

### Section 188. Extent of agent's authority

- An agent authorised to do an act has authority to do every lawful thing necessary to do that act.
- An agent authorised to carry on a business has authority to do every lawful thing necessary for that purpose, or usually done in conducting that business.

### Section 189. Agent's authority in an emergency

In an emergency, an agent has authority to do all acts to protect his principal from loss that a person of ordinary prudence would do in his own case in similar circumstances.

*Example:* An agent for sale holding perishable goods may, in an emergency, sell them to prevent their spoiling, if he cannot obtain instructions.

#### Sub-agents

### Section 190. When agent cannot delegate

An agent may not lawfully employ another to do acts he has expressly or impliedly undertaken to do personally, unless the ordinary custom of trade allows a sub-agent, or the nature of the agency requires one.

### Section 191. "Sub-agent" defined

A **sub-agent** is a person employed by, and acting under the control of, the original agent in the business of the agency.

### Section 192. Representation of principal by sub-agent properly appointed

- Where a sub-agent is properly appointed, the principal is, as regards third persons, represented by the sub-agent and is bound by and responsible for his acts as if he were an agent originally appointed by the principal.
- **Agent's responsibility for sub-agent:** the agent is responsible to the principal for the sub-agent's acts.
- **Sub-agent's responsibility:** the sub-agent is responsible for his acts to the agent, but not to the principal, except in cases of fraud or wilful wrong.

### Section 193. Agent's responsibility for sub-agent appointed without authority

Where an agent appoints a sub-agent without having authority to do so, the agent stands to that person as a principal to an agent, and is responsible for his acts both to the principal and to third persons. The principal is not represented by, or responsible for, that person, nor is that person responsible to the principal.

### Section 194. Relation between principal and person duly appointed by agent to act in business of agency

Where an agent who has express or implied authority to name another person to act for the principal does so, the person named is not a sub-agent but an agent of the principal for the part of the business entrusted to him.

*Example:* A directs B, his solicitor, to sell his estate by auction and to employ an auctioneer. B names C, an auctioneer. C is A's agent for the sale, not B's sub-agent.

### Section 195. Agent's duty in naming such person

In selecting such an agent for his principal, the agent must exercise the same discretion a person of ordinary prudence would exercise in his own case. If he does so, he is not responsible to the principal for the acts or negligence of the agent selected.

#### Ratification

### Section 196. Right of person as to acts done for him without his authority. Effect of ratification

Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another without that other's knowledge or authority, the other may choose to ratify or disown them. If he ratifies them, the same effects follow as if they had been done with his authority.

### Section 197. Ratification may be expressed or implied

Ratification may be express, or may be implied from the conduct of the person on whose behalf the acts were done.

*Example:* A, without authority, buys goods for B. B later sells them to C on his own account. B's conduct implies ratification of the purchase.

### Section 198. Knowledge requisite for valid ratification

A ratification is not valid if made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is materially defective.

### Section 199. Effect of ratifying unauthorised act forming part of a transaction

A person who ratifies an unauthorised act done on his behalf ratifies the whole transaction of which that act formed part.

### Section 200. Ratification of unauthorised act cannot injure third person

An act done by one person on behalf of another without authority, which, had it been authorised, would have exposed a third person to damages or ended a right or interest of a third person, cannot be given that effect by ratification.

*Example:* A holds a lease from B that can be ended on three months' notice. C, who has no authority from B, gives A notice ending the lease. B cannot ratify that notice so as to bind A.

#### Revocation of authority

### Section 201. Termination of agency

An agency ends:

- when the principal revokes the authority;
- when the agent renounces the business of the agency;
- when the business of the agency is completed;
- when either the principal or the agent dies or becomes of unsound mind; or
- when the principal is adjudicated insolvent under any law for the time being in force for the relief of insolvent debtors.

### Section 202. Termination of agency, where agent has an interest in subject-matter

Where the agent himself has an interest in the property that is the subject-matter of the agency, the agency cannot, without an express contract, be terminated to the prejudice of that interest.

*Example:* A gives B authority to sell A's land and pay himself, out of the proceeds, debts due to him from A. A cannot revoke this authority, nor is it ended by A's insanity or death.

### Section 203. When principal may revoke agent's authority

Subject to s. 202, the principal may revoke the agent's authority at any time before it has been exercised so as to bind the principal.

### Section 204. Revocation where authority has been partly exercised

The principal cannot revoke the authority after it has been partly exercised, so far as concerns acts and obligations arising from acts already done in the agency.

*Example:* A authorises B to buy 1,000 bales of cotton on A's account and pay for them out of A's money in B's hands. B buys the cotton in his own name, making himself personally liable. A cannot revoke B's authority so far as payment for the cotton is concerned.

### Section 205. Compensation for revocation by principal, or renunciation by agent

Where there is an express or implied contract that the agency will continue for a period of time, the principal must compensate the agent, or the agent the principal, for any earlier revocation or renunciation made without sufficient cause.

### Section 206. Notice of revocation or renunciation

Reasonable notice of revocation or renunciation must be given; otherwise the damage caused must be made good by the party at fault to the other.

### Section 207. Revocation and renunciation may be expressed or implied

Revocation or renunciation may be express, or may be implied from the conduct of the principal or agent respectively.

*Example:* A authorises B to let A's house. A later lets it himself. This is an implied revocation of B's authority.

### Section 208. When termination of agent's authority takes effect as to agent, and as to third persons

Termination of the agent's authority does not take effect as regards the agent until he knows of it, nor as regards third persons until they know of it.

*Example:* A directs B to sell goods for him and later, by letter, revokes the authority. B sells the goods before receiving the letter. The sale binds A.

### Section 209. Agent's duty on termination of agency by principal's death or insanity

When the agency ends because the principal dies or becomes of unsound mind, the agent must take all reasonable steps, on behalf of the late principal's representatives, to protect and preserve the interests entrusted to him.

### Section 210. Termination of sub-agent's authority

Termination of an agent's authority ends (subject to the rules on termination of an agent's authority) the authority of all sub-agents appointed by him.

#### Agent's duty to principal

### Section 211. Agent's duty in conducting principal's business

The agent must conduct the principal's business according to the principal's directions, or, without directions, according to the custom prevailing in that kind of business at the place where he conducts it. If he acts otherwise, he must make good any resulting loss to the principal, and account to him for any resulting profit.

### Section 212. Skill and diligence required from agent

- The agent must conduct the business with as much skill as is generally possessed by persons engaged in similar business, unless the principal knows of his lack of skill.
- He must always act with reasonable diligence and use the skill he has.
- He must compensate the principal for the direct consequences of his own neglect, lack of skill or misconduct, but not for loss or damage caused indirectly or remotely.

### Section 213. Agent's accounts

The agent must render proper accounts to the principal on demand.

### Section 214. Agent's duty to communicate with principal

In cases of difficulty, the agent must use all reasonable diligence to communicate with the principal and to obtain his instructions.

### Section 215. Right of principal when agent deals, on his own account, in business of agency without principal's consent

If the agent deals on his own account in the business of the agency without first obtaining the principal's consent and telling him all material circumstances known to the agent, the principal may repudiate the transaction if it is shown that the agent dishonestly concealed a material fact, or that the dealing was disadvantageous to the principal.

*Example:* A directs B to sell A's estate. B secretly buys it himself in another's name. A, on discovering this, may repudiate the sale if B dishonestly concealed a material fact or the sale was disadvantageous to A.

### Section 216. Principal's right to benefit gained by agent dealing on his own account in business of agency

If the agent, without the principal's knowledge, deals in the business of the agency on his own account instead of the principal's, the principal may claim from him any benefit the agent has obtained from the transaction.

### Section 217. Agent's right of retainer out of sums received on principal's account

The agent may retain, out of sums received on the principal's account in the business of the agency, all money due to himself for advances made or expenses properly incurred in conducting that business, and also the remuneration payable to him for acting as agent.

### Section 218. Agent's duty to pay sums received for principal

Subject to those deductions, the agent must pay the principal all sums received on his account.

### Section 219. When agent's remuneration becomes due

Unless there is a special contract, payment for performing an act is not due to the agent until the act is completed. However, the agent may keep money received for goods sold, even though not all the goods consigned to him have been sold, or the sale is not actually complete.

### Section 220. Agent not entitled to remuneration for business misconducted

An agent guilty of misconduct in the business of the agency is not entitled to remuneration for the part of the business he has misconducted.

### Section 221. Agent's lien on principal's property

Unless otherwise agreed, the agent may keep the principal's goods, papers and other property, movable or immovable, received by him, until the amount due to him for commission, disbursements and services in respect of them has been paid or accounted for.

#### Principal's duty to agent

### Section 222. Agent to be indemnified against consequences of lawful acts

The employer of an agent must indemnify him against the consequences of all lawful acts done by him in exercising the authority conferred on him.

### Section 223. Agent to be indemnified against consequences of acts done in good faith

Where one person employs another to do an act and the agent does it in good faith, the employer must indemnify the agent against its consequences, even if it injures the rights of third persons.

### Section 224. Non-liability of employer of agent to do a criminal act

Where one person employs another to do a criminal act, the employer is not liable, on any express or implied promise, to indemnify the agent against its consequences.

### Section 225. Compensation to agent for injury caused by principal's neglect

The principal must compensate the agent for injury caused to him by the principal's neglect or lack of skill.

*Example:* A employs B as a bricklayer to build a house and puts up the scaffolding himself. The scaffolding is unskilfully put up and B is hurt. A must compensate B.

#### Effect of agency on contracts with third persons

### Section 226. Enforcement and consequences of agent's contracts

Contracts made through an agent, and obligations arising from an agent's acts, may be enforced in the same way, and have the same legal consequences, as if the principal had made the contracts and done the acts himself.

### Section 227. Principal how far bound, when agent exceeds authority

When an agent does more than he is authorised to do, and the authorised part can be separated from the unauthorised part, only the authorised part binds the principal as between him and the agent's counterparty.

*Example:* A, owner of a ship and its cargo, authorises B to insure the ship for Rs. 4,000. B takes out one policy for that sum on the ship and a separate policy for the same sum on the cargo. A must pay the premium on the ship policy but not on the cargo policy.

### Section 228. Principal not bound when excess of agent's authority is not separable

Where an agent exceeds his authority and the excess cannot be separated from what is within his authority, the principal need not recognise the transaction.

*Example:* A authorises B to buy 500 sheep for him. B buys 500 sheep and 200 lambs for one combined price. A may repudiate the whole transaction.

### Section 229. Consequences of notice given to agent

Any notice given to, or information obtained by, the agent in the course of the business he transacts for the principal has, as between the principal and third parties, the same legal effect as if given to or obtained by the principal.

### Section 230. Agent cannot personally enforce, nor be bound by, contracts on behalf of principal

Without a contract to that effect, an agent cannot personally enforce contracts he makes on the principal's behalf, nor is he personally bound by them.

**Presumption of contract to contrary** — such a contract is presumed to exist:

- (1) where the agent contracts for the sale or purchase of goods for a merchant resident abroad;
- (2) where the agent does not disclose the name of his principal;
- (3) where the principal, though disclosed, cannot be sued.

### Section 231. Rights of parties to a contract made by agent not disclosed

- If an agent contracts with a person who neither knows nor has reason to suspect that he is an agent, the principal may require performance of the contract; but the other party has the same rights against the principal as he would have had against the agent if the agent had been the principal.
- If the principal reveals himself before the contract is completed, the other party may refuse to perform if he can show that he would not have entered into the contract had he known who the principal was, or that the agent was not the principal.

### Section 232. Performance of contract with agent supposed to be principal

Where a person contracts with another, neither knowing nor having reasonable ground to suspect that the other is an agent, the principal, if he requires performance, can obtain it only subject to the rights and obligations existing between the agent and the other party.

*Example:* A, who owes B Rs. 500, sells B Rs. 1,000 worth of rice. A is acting as agent for C, but B neither knows nor has reason to suspect this. C cannot compel B to take the rice without allowing B to set off A's debt.

### Section 233. Right of person dealing with agent personally liable

Where the agent is personally liable, a person dealing with him may hold the agent, the principal, or both liable.

*Example:* A enters into a contract with B to sell him 100 bales of cotton, and later discovers B was acting as agent for C. A may sue either B or C, or both, for the price.

### Section 234. Consequence of inducing agent or principal to act on belief that principal or agent will be held exclusively liable

A person who has contracted with an agent and induces the agent to act on the belief that only the principal will be held liable, or induces the principal to act on the belief that only the agent will be held liable, cannot afterwards hold the agent or the principal (respectively) liable.

### Section 235. Liability of pretended agent

A person who falsely represents himself to be another's authorised agent, and thereby induces a third person to deal with him as such, is liable — if the alleged principal does not ratify his acts — to compensate the third person for any loss or damage suffered by so dealing.

### Section 236. Person falsely contracting as agent not entitled to performance

A person who enters into a contract in the character of an agent cannot require its performance if he was really acting on his own account and not as an agent.

### Section 237. Liability of principal inducing belief that agent's unauthorized acts were authorized

When an agent, without authority, has done acts or incurred obligations to third persons on the principal's behalf, the principal is bound by them if he has, by his words or conduct, led those third persons to believe that the acts and obligations were within the agent's authority.

*Example:* A gives goods to B to sell and instructs him not to sell below a fixed price. C, unaware of the instructions, buys the goods from B for less. A is bound by the sale.

### Section 238. Effect, on agreement, of misrepresentation or fraud by agent

Misrepresentations made, or frauds committed, by agents acting in the course of their business for their principals affect agreements made by those agents in the same way as if the principals had made or committed them. But misrepresentations or frauds by agents in matters outside their authority do not affect the principals.

*Example:* A, being B's agent for selling goods, induces C to buy them by a misrepresentation that A was not authorised by B to make. The contract is voidable as between B and C at C's option.
